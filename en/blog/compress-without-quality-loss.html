How to Compress Images Without Losing Quality

How image compression works, what the quality setting really controls,

2026-09-17 · ~1100 words · 5 min read

"I compressed it and now it looks blurry." This article explains three things: why compression loses quality at all, how to tell how far you can push it, and which settings to use for which purpose — so you can pick a setting once instead of guessing.

1. Why compression always costs something

  • Lossless (PNG, lossless WebP) — rearranges data without dropping pixels, so the result is bit-identical to the original. The price: it barely shrinks (typically 10–30%, often less for photos).
  • Lossy (JPEG, lossy WebP) — discards information the human eye is insensitive to (high-frequency detail, colour precision). Typical savings: 70–95%. This is what almost every real use case wants.

The key insight: lossy compression does not blur everything evenly — it removes what your eye is least likely to notice. That is why quality 80 usually looks identical: what was dropped is exactly what you were ignoring anyway.

2. What the quality number actually controls

Quality 1–100 is not a "sharpness percentage" — it is quantisation precision. Lower values allow more aggressive merging of similar colours into one block.

80 is the widely accepted sweet spot: roughly a third of the original size, and most people cannot tell the difference at normal viewing distance.

3. The bigger lever: dimensions, not quality

This is the step most people skip. Pixel count affects file size far more than the quality setting.

File size is roughly proportional to pixel count. Shrinking 4000×3000 to 1920×1440 leaves 23% of the pixels — an automatic 77% saving, which matters far more than moving quality from 95 to 60.

Practical guidance:

  • Social media images → longest side 1080–1440 px is plenty (the platform re-compresses anyway)
  • In-article web images → 1200–1600 px (up to ~2000 px for retina)
  • Full-width hero images → 1920–2560 px
  • Printing → don't scale down; work backwards from DPI (A4 at 300 dpi needs ~3500 px)
  • Archiving only → don't compress at all. Keep the original.

The rule: compress to the width it will actually be displayed at. Keeping a phone-only image at 4000 px is pure waste.

6. Three common mistakes

Mistake 1: "Re-compressing is harmless"

It isn't — lossy damage is cumulative. JPEG → save as JPEG → save again layers artefacts on top of artefacts (called generation loss). Always compress once, from the original.

Mistake 2: "Higher quality is always better"

Quality 100 can be 2–3× the size of quality 85 with no perceptible gain. That's a lot of bytes for something nobody can see — and it directly slows page loads.

Mistake 3: "Compressing means resizing"

They're independent: you can lower quality without resizing, or resize without lowering quality. But resizing usually contributes far more to size reduction — so resize first, then tune quality.
